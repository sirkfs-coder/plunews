Why The Last God Reads Differently Than Most Fantasy Comics

If you’re used to fantasy comics with clear storylines and easy-to-follow lore, The Last God can feel like a shock. It doesn’t hand you obvious entry points. Instead, it throws you into a world packed with names, places, and mythologies, often before you know who’s who or what matters yet. The story jumps between two timelines, and the panels themselves are dense with detail. Trying to speed-read or rely on plot summaries later usually leads to confusion.

Illustrative example: On my first attempt, I tried to read half the first volume in one sitting. By the end of issue three, I’d mixed up characters and felt lost about who was alive in which timeline. When I slowed down, reading only one or two issues at a time and jotting down character names as they appeared, I started connecting the dots and actually enjoyed following the puzzle pieces as they fell into place.

Practical tip: Treat each issue as its own chapter. After finishing an issue, pause for a minute to note any new characters or important events before moving on. This small effort saves you from having to re-read when things get complicated later.

Navigating Dual Timelines Without Losing Track

The Last God’s two-timeline structure is both its biggest strength and its main hurdle. Scenes from the past echo through the present, but these connections aren’t always spelled out. Visual cues, like recurring symbols or changes in a location’s appearance, are often your only hints at how events tie together.

If you’re used to stories that explain everything through narration boxes, this approach can feel opaque. Skimming flashbacks or treating them as mere background almost guarantees you’ll miss out on why some characters act the way they do.

What helps: After each issue, pick one key scene that appears in both timelines (for example, the same fortress shown years apart). Compare details: Are banners different? Has the landscape changed? These clues tell you what history has altered, and help make sense of character motivations that aren’t spelled out directly.

How Much Lore Do You Really Need?

There’s a companion guide (Tales from the Book of Ages), but reading it before starting won’t necessarily make things easier. In fact, loading yourself up with all that extra background info early on can be overwhelming and distract from the emotional core of the story.

If you love deep lore and world-building for its own sake, dipping into the guide after every few issues can add flavor, but don’t treat it as required reading just to understand what’s happening.

Guideline: Move forward with the main story until you hit genuine confusion, a point where not knowing something blocks your enjoyment. Only then pause to check companion material for clarity. If you find yourself obsessively trying to memorize every name before continuing, take a step back: focus on following character arcs instead of cataloging every detail.

Moral Ambiguity Is Central, Not Just Decoration

Unlike many dark fantasy comics where bleakness is mostly for mood, The Last God makes moral ambiguity part of its structure. There isn’t a classic hero to root for; everyone is compromised by past choices or impossible situations.

If your main way into stories is finding someone “likable,” this series might feel cold at first. But if you shift focus, asking what survival costs these characters, or how their decisions echo across generations, you’ll likely find more reward.

Composite example: A friend almost gave up after issue two because she couldn’t find a favorite character. But once she started tracking moments when characters made tough choices (and reflecting on why those choices happened), she found herself far more invested, not because she liked anyone more, but because she wanted to see how their stories played out under pressure.

Action step: As you read, jot down scenes where someone faces an impossible choice or bends their morals. Looking back at these notes later makes it much easier to see how themes develop and keeps expectations realistic about what kind of story this is.

Should You Invest Your Time (and Money)?

The Last God isn’t cheap or quick, it takes both money (the collected editions are pricey) and mental energy due to its density. Before diving in:

  • Read Issue #1 digitally first.
  • If you’re intrigued by the atmosphere, even if some details confuse you, proceed with Volume 1.
  • If after two issues you still feel lost or disconnected, consider stopping; this structure just isn’t for everyone.
  • For collectors: Riccardo Federici’s art looks stunning in print editions; if visual detail matters most to you, physical copies are worth it.
  • If narrative depth is your main draw and cost is a concern, digital works fine.

What To Do If You’re Still Lost

If by issue two everything still feels tangled, even after taking notes, it’s okay to pause or walk away for now. The style isn’t universally appealing; don’t force yourself through frustration hoping it will suddenly “click.” Sometimes coming back later with fresh eyes helps; sometimes it doesn’t.

You can also try reading a summary online up through where you left off, see if filling in gaps gives enough context to continue enjoying the ride without memorizing every detail yourself.

Quickstart Checklist

  1. Sample Issue #1 digitally.
  2. Pause after each issue long enough to write down new character names or key places.
  3. Watch for repeated symbols or scenes across timelines, they matter.
  4. Don’t sweat over missing lore unless confusion stops your progress.
  5. Track tricky character choices; they’re central here.
  6. If lost after two issues despite trying these steps: give yourself permission to stop or come back later.

The Last God won’t work for everyone, but if you meet it halfway and let yourself move slowly, it offers an unusually rich dark fantasy experience that rewards close attention and patience.
