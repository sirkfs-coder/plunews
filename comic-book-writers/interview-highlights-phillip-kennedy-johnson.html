Getting to talk with a writer like Phillip Kennedy Johnson is a real gift if you love comics, especially if you want to understand why some stories stick with readers for years. But turning those conversations into sharp, honest highlights? That’s where things get tricky. It’s easy to fall into old traps, especially when the talk is all about “character-driven storytelling.” I’ve stumbled through those traps myself and learned a few hard lessons worth sharing.

Don’t Let “Character First” Flatten the Story

Johnson is famous for rooting every plot beat in deep character values. This sounds perfect: know your hero, know your story. But when I first tried to capture this in an interview recap, I made his philosophy sound more rigid than it really was. I focused so much on his mantra (“Superman is hope,” he’d say) that I left out all the messy push and pull behind the scenes.

What I wish I’d done: Instead of just repeating the core value, pause and ask: Am I showing how Johnson actually works through situations where character ideals bump up against other demands? For example, he’s talked about moments when editorial direction or licensing rules forced him to rethink a character’s action, even when it went against his instincts. That tension tells you far more about the creative process than another quote about “truth.”

Illustrative example: In one piece, I kept circling back to Superman-as-hope. By draft three, it read like a fan letter instead of an honest look at how Johnson makes choices under pressure. Only after talking it over with another editor did I realize what was missing: evidence of struggle and compromise.

Show Contradictions, They’re Where Growth Happens

It’s tempting to smooth out any mixed messages for clarity’s sake. But real interviews are messy, and creators like Johnson often contradict themselves as they work through tough problems.

He’s shared (sometimes reluctantly) that he’s rewritten entire arcs after getting late-stage feedback from higher-ups or partners on a project. These moments, where he bends or even breaks his own rules, are gold if you want readers to see him as a working artist, not just a quote machine.

Quick check: Before finalizing your highlights, flag any moment where Johnson seems to hesitate or double back on himself. Those are usually signals of something real and revealing, not mistakes to edit out.

Use Context Wisely, Don’t Smother Good Quotes

Early on, I thought every highlight needed loads of setup (“He said this after a long meeting with…”). Too much context kills momentum and buries the punch of what matters.

The fix: Add context only where leaving it out would change how someone reads the quote or make it misleading. If Johnson references an industry controversy or creative disagreement, give readers enough background to follow along, but let straightforward insights stand on their own.

If you’re unsure: Read your draft aloud. If you find yourself getting lost in tangents before each quote, trim until only what truly matters remains.

Choose Fresh Material Over Familiar Soundbites

Fans expect certain themes (“Superman inspires hope,” “heroes are flawed”). But if every highlight echoes past interviews, your article will blend in with dozens of others.

What works better: Hunt for moments where Johnson shares uncertainty or surprise, maybe he admits to imposter syndrome or talks about scrapping an outline he loved because something new sparked during scripting. Scan recent interviews side by side with your own notes; if most of your best material shows up elsewhere, dig deeper for something unique from your conversation.

Protect Against Misrepresentation (Especially Under Deadline)

Shortcuts can backfire fast when time is tight. Once, rushing So, what's the takeaway? a complicated answer about editorial influence, I nearly made Johnson sound frustrated when he was actually just being honest about industry realities. Luckily, checking my notes against the audio saved me from publishing something misleading, a mistake that could have cost trust.

Simple safeguards:

  • Always check direct quotes twice.
  • Paraphrase only if you’re sure of intent.
  • When in doubt about meaning or tone, ask for clarification before going live.

Sometimes Leaving Out Details Is More Honest

Not every great story belongs in your highlights reel, even if it’s amusing or personal. Too many side anecdotes can distract from what makes Johnson unique as a creator.

Here’s how I decide what stays:

  • Does this detail actually illuminate his approach?
  • Is it exclusive to my interview?
  • Would its absence change how readers see him?

If not, cut or reframe it as brief color instead of making it a headline moment.

One Concrete Step That Makes Articles Better

Before hitting “publish,” look over your highlights again and ask: Where am I glossing over real-world friction? Where do ideals meet obstacles? Revise at least one section to show that collision, whether it’s an editorial demand that forced a rewrite or a moment where Johnson had to pick between two imperfect options.

Adding even one note of honest struggle or contradiction will make your piece resonate more, and help readers see both the inspiration and reality behind great comics writing.

There’s no formula for perfect interview highlights. But by staying alert for complexity (not just slogans), double-checking context and accuracy, and choosing fresh angles over familiar ones, you’ll do justice both to creators like Phillip Kennedy Johnson and your own voice as an interviewer. And honestly, that’s where the fun starts.
