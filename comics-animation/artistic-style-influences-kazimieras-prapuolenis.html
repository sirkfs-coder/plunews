Kazimieras Prapuolenis (Kaz): What Makes His Art So Distinct, and How His Influences Actually Work

Kaz’s comics, especially his long-running strip "Underworld," have a way of sticking in your mind: they’re playful, unnerving, and instantly recognizable. You don’t just see a Kaz panel, you feel it, like walking into a familiar dream that’s started to bend at the edges.

Let’s break down exactly how Kaz arrives at this effect, why simple imitation falls flat, and how you can actually learn from his approach.

Clear Cartoon Logic Collides with Urban Absurdity

Kaz’s style has roots in classic American cartooning, but he warps those roots until they’re almost unrecognizable. He uses:

  • Bold, confident lines: Think of the sturdy outlines in George Herriman’s "Krazy Kat" or E.C. Segar’s "Popeye." Kaz draws with the same kind of authority; every shape is clear and direct.
  • Elastic anatomy: Bodies, faces, even objects stretch and contort for comedic payoff. In one "Underworld" strip, a character’s mouth curves all the way around their head just to deliver a punchline.
  • Urban clutter that sets mood: Backgrounds aren’t generic cityscapes, they’re full of dumpsters, peeling posters, stray cats. But every detail has a job: sometimes the setting delivers the joke (like an alley wall covered in failed motivational graffiti), sometimes it just anchors the weirdness in something solid.

What really trips up people trying to copy Kaz is thinking it’s just about being “weird.” In reality, his world runs on strict cartoon logic. Even the oddest moments are grounded by readable staging and timing.

Illustrative Example: Why Copying Weirdness Fails

When I first tried drawing something “in the style of Kaz,” I loaded my panel with bizarre characters, an eyeball with legs here, a vending machine full of bees there, assuming more absurdity meant more humor. Instead, my comic was confusing and exhausting to look at. Going back to actual "Underworld" strips taught me what I was missing: Kaz never lets chaos overwhelm structure. If there’s a talking mailbox or existential rat, it exists for a reason, usually as set-up or punchline.

His Actual Influences, And Where Most Analyses Go Shallow

People often say Kaz mixes early newspaper comics with underground comix, but that’s only part of it.

  • Visual storytelling: From Herriman ("Krazy Kat"), Kaz borrows strong silhouettes and instantly readable shapes.
  • Surrealism and subversion: From Robert Crumb and other underground cartoonists, he gets license to push subject matter into stranger (sometimes darker) territory, think vending machines dispensing clouds of flies instead of snacks.
  • Timing and anti-jokes: The best punchlines aren’t always quippy; sometimes an awkward pause or blank stare says everything. This echoes Jules Feiffer or Charles Schulz (the later years).

The trap most imitators fall into: focusing only on one influence (drawing clean shapes without bite) or getting lost in grotesquerie (piling on bizarre details without structure). Kaz always balances both sides.

How to Really Study Kaz, A Step-by-Step Approach

If you want to understand, or emulate, Kaz’s style:

  1. Analyze Function First
  • For each element in a panel, ask: does this serve the joke? Is this trash can just background clutter, or does it reinforce the mood?
  1. Spot Where Expectations Flip
  • Look for that moment where things go sideways, a vending machine that dispenses not food but existential dread; a character who walks off mid-conversation.
  1. Notice Restraint
  • Some panels are surprisingly spare. Awkward silences or empty alleys do as much work as wordy dialogue.

Before you try drawing your own “Kaz-style” comic, grab three "Underworld" strips and run through those questions for each one. Write out what each visual choice accomplishes, not just what looks odd.

Quick Reference: The Essentials of Kaz’s Style

  • Bold lines that make every character pop
  • Cartoon physics: bodies and objects flex for humor
  • Urban settings that feel lived-in but never cluttered for their own sake
  • Jokes that end on an unresolved note, a sigh or anticlimax instead of a punchline
  • Every weird detail earns its place by serving either setup or payoff

What NOT To Do When Learning from Kaz

  • Don’t pile on surreal details without clear structure, it kills readability.
  • Don’t force punchlines, sometimes resignation is funnier than wackiness.
  • Don’t mistake strangeness for intent, the real magic is how chaos is anchored by discipline.

Encouragement for New Artists (or Writers)

You might worry your version won’t be “strange enough” or that you’ll miss something subtle, but remember: Kaz builds his world from clarity first. It’s okay if your first attempts feel too plain; focus on making every element serve its purpose before amping up weirdness.

Even if comics aren’t your end goal, studying how Kaz mixes structure with unpredictability will sharpen your eye for what makes visual storytelling work anywhere.

If you want to go deeper: Pick one favorite "Underworld" panel today. Before redrawing it, or writing about it, jot down what makes it land emotionally. That moment of explanation is where real understanding starts.

Kaz doesn’t just draw weird worlds, he engineers them carefully so you never get lost in nonsense. That’s what gives his work its staying power, and why simply copying surface oddities will always miss what makes him great.
