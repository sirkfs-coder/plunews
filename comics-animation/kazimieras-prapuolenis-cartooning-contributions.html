Kazimieras Prapuolenis (Kaz): How One Cartoonist Changed the Rules

Kazimieras Prapuolenis, usually just “Kaz”, didn’t quietly fit into the world of cartooning. He barged in, rewired how comics could work, and left fingerprints all over the medium. If you want to understand his impact, don’t just count his credits: look at what actually shifted after he started publishing.

Disrupting Routine: What Made “Underworld” Different?

Most daily comics follow a safe formula. They’re quick to read, easy on the eyes, and rarely ask you to stop and think. “Underworld,” Kaz’s long-running strip for alternative weeklies, broke those habits. His panels are crowded with grotesque, lopsided characters, unpredictable gags, and backgrounds stuffed with odd details. It’s not just weird for weirdness’ sake, it snaps you out of autopilot.

Specific example:
Try reading the “Underworld” strip where Smoking Cat wanders through a landscape of melting clocks and talking sandwiches (first published in 1995). At first glance, it feels chaotic. But if you slow down, subtle punchlines emerge from the background graffiti or a character’s one-off expression. Most comic strips aim for instant clarity; Kaz’s invite you to linger, and reward you if you do.

Why this matters:
Editors in the early 90s had to decide: Do we risk confusing our readers by running something this strange? Some did, and discovered that bored audiences were ready for something challenging. After “Underworld” found its footing, other alt-weekly editors became more open to comics that didn’t play it safe.

Pushing Animation Forward: Taking Risks at Nickelodeon

Kaz didn’t stay underground. When he moved into animation, especially as a writer and storyboard artist for “SpongeBob SquarePants”, he brought his taste for oddball humor and visual surprise with him.

Concrete incident (composite example):
During development of a SpongeBob episode in the early 2000s, Kaz pitched a scene where time seems to loop: SpongeBob repeats an action three times in rapid succession until Squidward yells in confusion. The director hesitated (“Is this too jarring?”), but staff who knew Kaz’s strip argued that these moments made episodes memorable. The gag stayed in, and fans still point out those offbeat sequences as favorites.

What changed:
Producers noticed that when they allowed these wild ideas through, not just random weirdness, but jokes built on their own logic, audiences responded with enthusiasm. Over time, more animators felt emboldened to suggest unconventional story beats or visual gags, knowing there was now proof this approach worked.

Pattern Violation: Why Kaz’s Style Sticks

Kaz’s characters are never symmetrical; their faces squiggle, their bodies twist unexpectedly. Cognitive psychology calls this the Von Restorff effect: unusual things stick in your memory better than ordinary ones.

In practice:
Look at any two-panel Underworld strip: chances are there’s at least one sight gag buried behind the main action, a tiny monster eating another character’s hat or a background sign offering “Free Regret.” You might miss it on your first read but spot it later. This density means readers keep coming back, not because they’re confused, but because there’s always more to find.

For creators considering this path:
Kaz’s dense style works best where there’s room for re-reading or re-watching (like alternative weeklies or cult TV shows). In mass-market newspapers or mainstream kids’ animation, editors may see it as clutter unless someone champions it internally.

Influence Spreads by Example (and Sometimes Misfire)

The clearest evidence of Kaz’s impact isn’t just homage, it’s editors taking bigger risks and artists stretching further after seeing what audiences embraced.

Composite example:
After “Underworld” gained traction, other cartoonists tried ramping up their own weirdness, sometimes without enough structure underneath. One alt-weekly editor recalled getting flooded with strips full of bizarre characters but no clear joke or narrative thread. Readers got lost; editors pulled those strips quickly. The lesson? Kaz’s chaos works because there is always an internal logic, his punchlines connect back to the setup even if the road is winding.

The Trade-Offs Every Creator Faces

Kaz made choices that sometimes limited his reach, but preserved his voice. He thrived in spaces that rewarded originality (alt-weeklies, certain animation studios), not places demanding universal appeal.

  • If you chase pure uniqueness without thinking about your audience or venue, you risk alienating both.
  • If you water down your style too much, your work becomes forgettable.
  • Editors need evidence before they’ll bet on something risky; Kaz provided that proof through repeated successes.

Try This Yourself: How to Analyze Kaz's Methods

If you want to really understand how Kaz breaks expectations:

  1. Pick two Underworld strips from different years (say, 1994 and 2004). Note exactly where he disrupts routine, maybe it’s a strange layout or an unexpected punchline buried in graffiti.
  2. Watch an animated segment he worked on (for example, the “Rock Bottom” episode from SpongeBob). Pause when things get strange: does it confuse you? Make you laugh? Make you curious?
  3. Share with a friend who doesn’t know Kaz and compare reactions, do they feel thrown off or drawn in?
  4. Ask yourself: Did breaking the pattern make the joke land harder? Did it stick with you afterward?

This process will give you direct insight into why his style works, and where imitators often miss the mark by copying only surface-level weirdness instead of underlying logic.

Kaz once said he wanted comics that made people stop and puzzle over them, a little friction as an invitation rather than a barrier. If you find yourself pausing mid-strip or rewinding an animated gag just to catch everything going on beneath the surface…that’s not an accident. That’s Kaz at work, and his influence lives on every time someone dares to break a pattern instead of following one blindly.
