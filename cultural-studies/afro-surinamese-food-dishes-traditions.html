Afro-Surinamese Cooking: Common Pitfalls and How to Fix Them

Cooking Afro-Surinamese food at home can be deeply rewarding, especially when you get those rich, comforting flavors just right. But in my own kitchen (and from watching friends), I’ve learned this cuisine offers unique challenges. Here’s how to recognize the most common issues with classics like pom and moksi alesi, along with practical ways to fix them, so you end up with food that’s true to its roots and tastes fantastic.

Pom: Soggy, Dry, or Just Off? Fixing Suriname’s Favorite Party Dish

Pom is the baked heart of many Surinamese celebrations. Its golden crust hides a soft interior made from pomtayer (a starchy root) mixed with citrus juice and a savory chicken filling. But pom goes wrong easily, either watery under the surface or dry as cardboard.

  • What typically goes wrong:
  • Using frozen or water-heavy taro as a substitute for pomtayer and not squeezing out extra moisture.
  • Adding all the liquid a recipe calls for without checking the texture.
  • Relying on bottled or canned citrus juice, which can be both too acidic and too watery.

How to check as you go:
After grating your root vegetable, grab a handful and squeeze hard. If it drips water, wrap it in a clean towel and squeeze out as much liquid as you can. When adding citrus or broth, pour in about half first. The mixture should be thick but not crumbly, think stiff mashed potatoes.

If you realize it's too wet before baking:
Fold in more grated root if possible. If it’s already in the oven and looks like soup underneath, lay foil loosely over the top, turn down the heat by 20°C (about 35°F), and let it bake longer so it sets up.

Moksi Alesi: Avoiding Tough Meat and Salty Rice

Moksi alesi is Surinamese comfort food: rice cooked in one pot with beans, smoked or salted meat (like beef or fish), herbs, and sometimes coconut milk. It sounds simple, but I’ve seen people struggle with chewy meat or rice that tastes overwhelmingly salty.

  • Common mistakes:
  • Failing to soak salted beef long enough to remove excess salt.
  • Skipping parboiling of tough meats before adding them to the rice.
  • Using pre-cooked meats without tasting for salt levels first.

A quick test:
After soaking your salted meat (ideally for several hours, changing the water once or twice), cut off a piece and simmer separately for about 15 minutes. Taste it; it should be flavorful but not overpowering. If it’s still too salty or tough, soak again or simmer longer before using it in your dish.

Illustrative example:
A friend once used store-bought corned beef straight from the package, her moksi alesi was nearly inedible! She salvaged it by rinsing the shredded beef under warm water before mixing in. Not perfect, but much better than tossing the whole pot.

Rice Texture: Getting Fluffy Instead of Mushy or Hard

Good moksi alesi means each grain of rice is tender but separate, not mushy porridge nor hard pellets mixed into stew.

  • Where things go wrong:
  • Adding all ingredients at once so some cook faster than others.
  • Not adjusting water if using canned beans (which bring their own liquid).
  • Picking “quick-cook” rice varieties that fall apart or clump up under long cooking.

What works best:
After sautéing your aromatics and prepped meat (and soaked beans if using dry), add rinsed rice and just enough liquid to barely cover everything, about an inch above the solids is a good rule of thumb. Check after 20 minutes; if there’s still too much liquid but the rice is softening, uncover for a few minutes so steam escapes. If it’s drying out but still firm inside, sprinkle in hot broth little by little until done.

Ingredient Swaps: Substitutes Without Sacrificing Flavor

Outside Suriname, you might have to make do, but some swaps work better than others.

  • Pitfalls I’ve run into:
  • Waxier potatoes instead of taro make pom gluey rather than fluffy.
  • Canned beans dumped straight from the can taste flat compared to those simmered briefly with seasonings.
  • Smoked turkey instead of salted beef changes both texture and saltiness; without care, one flavor overwhelms everything else.

How to adapt thoughtfully:
If substituting roots for pomtayer, choose ones low in moisture and high in starch (taro over potato). For proteins like smoked turkey, always taste both side by side, a pinch of salt may be needed if your swap is bland; less seasoning if your substitute is salty already. And always rinse canned beans thoroughly before simmering them with broth for deeper flavor before adding them to your main dish.

Respecting Tradition: Names and Customs Matter

Food isn’t just technique, it carries names and rituals that mean something special within community celebrations. Renaming dishes (“Suriname casserole” instead of “pom”), skipping traditional serving steps (such as ancestor libations at holiday tables), or combining customs without context dulls what makes these meals unique.

If adapting for new friends:

  • Use original dish names where possible.
  • Briefly explain any big changes you’re making, and why.
  • Acknowledge sources: if you learned from someone’s grandmother rather than a website recipe collection, mention that connection!

As someone who learned these dishes from neighbors sharing stories over shared meals, I know how much these details matter, not just for flavor but for feeling connected across generations.

Your Next Step: Cook Twice With Curiosity

Ready to really learn? Try making moksi alesi or pom twice, first strictly by recipe, then again while paying attention to these diagnostic checks along the way. The second time through, note where things felt different (maybe you squeezed more moisture from taro or adjusted salt after tasting). These small tweaks build real confidence, and every batch gets closer to home.

Cooking Afro-Surinamese food well takes patience and care, but every attempt brings more understanding of its traditions and joys. If something goes awry one night? You’re not alone; even seasoned cooks keep learning!
