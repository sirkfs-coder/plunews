Most advice about supporting activists with dwarfism stops at “share their stories” or “follow them online.” That’s well-meaning, but if you want to move beyond surface-level support, it takes a lot more. I’ve spent years working alongside disability advocates and consulting on accessibility, and I’ve seen how quickly good intentions can fizzle out when bigger systems aren’t willing to shift. Here’s how to make your support count, and ensure the voices and leadership of people with dwarfism actually shape the world around us.

Why Awareness Alone Leaves Advocates Stuck

Early in my career, I led a disability rights workshop featuring several speakers with dwarfism. Attendees clapped and felt inspired. But afterward, someone asked: “Why weren’t any of these leaders involved in planning or decision-making for this event?” That question hit hard. Featuring someone isn’t the same as giving them real authority or resources.

What holds real change back?

  • Most organizations highlight athletes or celebrities, people who already have a platform.
  • Leaders pushing for policy change often get overlooked because they aren’t as visible.
  • Decision-making roles remain out of reach due to inaccessible structures or old habits.

Shifting Focus: From Personal Stories to Systemic Action

If you’re organizing an event, writing an article, or starting a campaign, ask yourself: Am I spotlighting individual triumphs, or showing how people are changing systems?

Stronger ways to feature advocates:

  • Highlight work that shifts policies: For example, Sinéad Burke advises governments on design standards so buildings become more accessible for everyone. Becky Curran Kekula works with companies to reform hiring practices and promote authentic representation.
  • Explain the “how”: Don’t just say what someone believes, show how they build coalitions, negotiate for better access, or help re-write company rules.
  • Acknowledge obstacles: Is the person invited to speak, but never brought into leadership? Are venues still inaccessible?

Ask yourself before inviting or featuring someone:

  • Is this advocate influencing decisions (budgets, hiring) or only sharing personal experiences?
  • What measurable changes have they driven, laws passed, new company policies?
  • Can you document these changes?

When Good Intentions Hit a Wall: An Illustrative Example

Here’s a composite example inspired by several real-life situations. An activist with dwarfism was often asked to tell her story at conferences but was never included in program design. Her speeches got standing ovations but didn’t lead to improvements, no ramps added, no leadership roles offered. It wasn’t until she teamed up with others across the disability community and pushed for direct involvement that doors finally opened. Only after persistent requests did organizers invite her into actual planning meetings.

What does this reveal?
It’s much simpler (and safer) for organizations to book an inspirational speaker than it is to share decision-making power. Shifting that balance can feel risky, but it’s where genuine inclusion begins.

Common Pitfall: Amplifying Voices Without Backing Them Up

Sharing TED Talks or social media posts is easy, and sometimes necessary, but without follow-through, it rarely leads anywhere.

A typical scenario:
A manager shares Sinéad Burke’s talk at a staff meeting. Everyone feels moved, for a day. But unless this energy turns into reviewing job descriptions for bias or updating physical spaces for accessibility, nothing actually changes.

How to make amplification matter:
Anytime you highlight an advocate, match their story with a concrete next step, like launching a policy review or inviting them into process improvements (with pay and real input).

How to Choose Advocates Without Slipping Into Tokenism

To avoid surface-level representation:

  • Look at their impact: Have they helped change laws or organizational practices?
  • Seek self-authored sources: Use first-person essays, interviews, and official bios over secondhand news articles.
  • Balance visibility: Don’t just focus on famous names; local leaders who’ve organized accessibility audits or steered grassroots campaigns matter too.
  • Follow up regularly: Leadership evolves. Revisit profiles every year instead of treating advocacy as one-time recognition.

Measuring Impact: What Actually Signals Real Change?

When featuring advocates, or deciding how best to support them, look for evidence such as:

  • Board seats or advisory roles with actual influence
  • Specific examples of policy shifts linked back to their efforts
  • Track records of mentoring others into leadership
  • Their work being referenced by other organizations as a model

If you can say yes even once here, you’re likely looking at someone whose work goes deeper than awareness.

Your Next Step: Move From Inspiration To Action

If you want your involvement to matter:

  1. Pick one area under your control (event planning, hiring policies, curriculum).
  2. Invite an advocate with lived experience of dwarfism to review it directly alongside you, not just as a guest speaker.
  3. Pay them for their expertise and give them genuine influence over decisions.
  4. Track, and share, what changes as a result, even if progress feels slow.

That’s how inspiration becomes structural change, and where true inclusion starts growing roots.

Cheering each other on matters, but backing that up with shared power is where we see real results. If you’re ready to take action and unsure where to begin, try reaching out locally first; grassroots leaders often know exactly what needs fixing next. And remember: every small step toward genuine partnership opens the door wider for everyone who comes after.
