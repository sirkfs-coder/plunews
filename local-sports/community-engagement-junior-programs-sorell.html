Junior Programs: Growth Off the Field as Much as On

When my son first joined the Under-9s, he barely knew which way to run. I expected he’d learn the rules and hopefully enjoy himself. What I didn’t expect was how quickly he found his place. The coaches keep things straightforward: every kid gets a go, no matter their skill level; new faces are buddied up with someone who’s been around longer; encouragement is louder than criticism.

A typical week isn’t just drills and sprints. One evening I watched the coach pause training because a player was upset after missing a goal. Instead of moving on, he gathered everyone and talked about backing each other up, and within minutes, they were all laughing again. That kind of support sticks.

Key details that make it work:

  • No experience needed for kids or parents, showing up is enough.
  • Simple routines help kids settle in fast.
  • Every child plays each week, so no one feels left out or sidelined.

If you’re thinking your child might be too shy or inexperienced: give it one session. Most start exactly that way.

Community Engagement: Real Connections Start Small

It’s easy to think community engagement means fundraising drives or big events. At Sorell, it often starts with something simple, a Family Day sausage sizzle or a midweek coffee at the ground while the kids train.

Some of the best friendships I’ve made began over burnt onions and ketchup bottles at the canteen table. At last year’s holiday clinic, for example, we had a muddy relay race between coaches and Under-13s. Everyone ended up filthy and laughing, including parents who hadn’t spoken before that day.

What actually brings people together here:

  • Family Days where anyone can join in regardless of club membership.
  • School clinics run by volunteer coaches so local kids can try footy without pressure.
  • Charity matches that become afternoons of shared stories as much as sport.

Illustrative example: Two families moved to Sorell last winter and turned up just to watch a Family Day match. By season’s end they were regulars, one mum ran uniform orders, another dad organized carpools for away games.

The first step is always showing up, even if you don’t know anyone yet.

Volunteering: No Experience Needed, Just Join In

A lot of parents worry they don’t know enough about footy to help out. Truth is, most roles involve practical jobs like setting up cones or handing out oranges at halftime, not running drills or making tactical decisions.

Easy ways to get involved:

  • Help set up equipment before training (five minutes makes a big difference).
  • Take an hour at the canteen during matches.
  • Offer to help post updates on social media or manage team lists.

You don’t need any background in football; most volunteers started with zero experience. The junior coordinator pairs new helpers with someone who knows the ropes. If you want to stay in the background for your first few weeks, that’s fine too.

Composite example: One dad started by collecting boundary flags after games because his daughter was nervous unless he was close by. By winter break he was helping check scoresheets, and still laughs about not knowing half the rules when he started.

Removing Barriers: How Sorell Welcomes New Families

Plenty of families hesitate because they worry about cost, getting to games, or feeling out of place. Sorell tackles these head-on:

  • Cost: Quiet payment plans and grants are available; just ask a committee member, no questions or fuss.
  • Transport: Carpool lists get set up at registration time; opt in whenever you need it.
  • Settling in: New players get matched with buddies on their first night; new volunteers shadow someone friendly until they’re comfortable.
  • Time: Training runs 60–90 minutes after school; weekend matches are scheduled around school events when possible.

If something feels tricky, just mention it at training or message through the club page. Chances are there’s already an answer ready.

What Joining Looks Like Week-to-Week

Here’s what you can expect if your family decides to give it a go:

  • Auskick (ages 5–8): One playful session per week focused on fun and movement
  • Under 9s–17s: One or two weekday trainings (after school), then matches on Saturday mornings at Pembroke Park or nearby grounds
  • Girls’ teams: Same structure; numbers have grown so quickly there are now separate squads and dedicated coaches

Holiday clinics pop up for skills like tackling or goal kicking, last year’s mudslide relay is still talked about by half the Under-13s!

Around 120 juniors played last season with more than 30 parent volunteers involved, the club’s big enough for energy but small enough that everyone gets noticed.

The Real Value: Lasting Friendships and Confidence

The biggest surprise isn’t how much kids improve, it’s how quickly families feel part of something real. Local businesses sponsor teams because their own children play here; shy kids end up leading warm-ups; parents find themselves cheering not just for their own but everyone else’s child too.

If you’re waiting until you know more before turning up, you’ll wait forever! Most families come along once “just to see” and find themselves signing up because it feels welcoming from day one.

Ready to Try? Here’s How To Start This Week

You don’t need to plan everything out ahead of time. Just turn up at Pembroke Park for any junior training session (see times on the club website), introduce yourself to someone in club gear, they’ll point you in the right direction straight away.

If you want details before visiting:

  1. Check sorellfc.com.au/juniors for session times
  2. Message via Facebook (“Sorell Football Club Juniors”) for quick answers
  3. Or simply show up on Thursday after school, someone will greet you at the gate

That first visit is all you need, the rest happens naturally once you’re there.
