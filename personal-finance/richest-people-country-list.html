Richest People by Country: Who Holds the Top Spot in Each Nation?

If you came here looking for a quick, definitive table of the richest person in every country, you’re not alone. It’s a common request, and one that’s surprisingly tricky to answer with confidence. The process is less about finding a single “correct” list and more about understanding why the answer keeps changing, what counts as someone’s “country,” and how to avoid getting tripped up by unreliable data.

Below, I’ll walk through what actually works when you need this information, highlight real-world snags, and give you a practical template for building (or checking) your own list. I’ll also include some specific examples so you can see the process in action.

Why No Two Lists Ever Match: The Core Challenges

1. Defining “Country” Isn’t Simple

Is Elon Musk South African, Canadian, or American? It depends who you ask and when. Forbes might count birthplace or citizenship; Bloomberg might use main residence or business activity. Some billionaires hold multiple passports or move frequently for tax reasons. This means the same person can appear on different countries’ lists, or disappear altogether, depending on the source.

2. Fortunes Change Fast

Stock prices swing daily. In 2023, Gautam Adani lost tens of billions in days after negative press coverage, briefly dropping him below Mukesh Ambani as India’s richest person before partially rebounding weeks later. These shifts aren’t rare outliers; they happen all the time near the top of these rankings.

3. Some Fortunes Stay Hidden

In smaller economies or countries with less financial transparency, it’s harder to estimate private wealth accurately. Not every billionaire is public-facing; family fortunes held through trusts or offshore companies often go unreported unless there’s an IPO or major sale.

Composite Example: When a List Blows Up Mid-Presentation

A colleague once prepped slides for a global finance summit showing each G20 nation’s richest individual. She pulled most names from Forbes but double-checked controversial cases with Bloomberg’s live tracker. Even so, her “India” slide was out of date before she finished rehearsing, Adani overtook Ambani overnight due to market swings, but not all sources updated at the same speed.

During Q&A, several attendees asked how she chose which version to trust. She realized too late that she hadn’t documented her criteria, leading to confusion and a scramble to justify her choices on the spot.

The lesson: If your audience cares about accuracy (and most do), keeping notes on where and when you got each data point matters just as much as the numbers themselves.

How To Build a Reliable “Richest By Country” List

If you want results people can trust, or just don’t want to get caught flat-footed, follow this approach:

1. Decide Your Purpose

  • For an academic paper? Be extra strict on documentation.
  • For a news story? Use one reputable source and timestamp it.
  • For curiosity? Note that any answer is temporary.

2. Pick One Source Per List
Choose either Forbes’ Billionaires List or Bloomberg’s Billionaires Index. Mixing them usually creates more confusion than clarity unless you’re prepared to explain differences for each country.

3. Always Record These Details
For each country:

  • Name of individual
  • Net worth (in USD)
  • Main source of wealth/industry
  • How their country is defined (citizenship, residence, etc.)
  • Source URL
  • Date accessed

Example entry:

CountryNameNet WorthIndustryNationality BasisSourceDate
IndiaMukesh Ambani$95BEnergyCitizenshipForbes URL2024-06-05

4. Flag Close Contests and Uncertainties
If sources disagree (common in places like India or France), add a note:
“Net worths within $500M as of June 2024; ranking may change day-to-day.”

5. Don’t Invent Data for Gaps
Some countries have no billionaires listed anywhere (e.g., many small island nations). Just write “no billionaire reported as of [date].”

Specific Examples: How This Works Right Now

Here are three sample entries using Forbes’ listings as of June 2024:

  • United States:
    Elon Musk ($210B; tech/automotive), listed under US citizenship by Forbes.
  • France:
    Bernard Arnault ($196B; luxury goods), French citizenship.
  • Nigeria:
    Aliko Dangote ($14B; cement/agriculture), Nigerian citizenship.

Notice: If you check Bloomberg today instead, net worths, and sometimes even rankings, will differ slightly because they update more frequently based on market movements.

Common Pitfalls To Avoid

When checking someone else’s list, or reviewing your own draft, watch out for these warning signs:

  • No source or date attached to figures.
  • A name appears that doesn’t show up on either Forbes or Bloomberg in recent months.
  • Including someone whose main business is abroad (for example, listing someone as Brazil’s richest when they live full-time in Switzerland).
  • Assigning someone to multiple countries without explaining why.

If any of these pop up, pause and double-check before relying on that information.

When Data Conflicts: What To Do

Conflicting info isn’t just common, it’s expected! Here’s how to handle it transparently:

  1. Choose which source takes priority (and explain why).
  2. If two people are close together in net worth, list both, with their numbers and dates, so readers see exactly where things stand.
  3. For dual citizens or those with unclear ties, stick with your chosen source's assignment but add a short note if readers might expect them elsewhere (“Elon Musk was born in South Africa but appears under United States due to primary business activity”).

Ready-To-Go Template For Your Own Research

You don’t need fancy tools, a simple spreadsheet will do:
Columns: Country | Name | Net Worth (USD) | Industry/Source of Wealth | Nationality Basis | Source URL | Date Accessed | Notes

Each time you update your list, fill in these fields directly from your chosen source(s). This makes your work easy to defend, even if fortunes change tomorrow.

Encouragement: Transparency Beats Perfection

It’s tempting to chase after a perfect “richest by country” list, but even experts can’t freeze those numbers for long because fortunes shift constantly and definitions keep evolving behind the scenes.

What matters most is being open about where your data comes from and what limits it has at that moment in time. If you follow these steps, and keep good notes, you’ll be ahead of almost everyone else working with global wealth rankings.

If you hit a snag with conflicting info or missing data for a particular country, just say so clearly; honesty builds trust far faster than guessing ever could.
