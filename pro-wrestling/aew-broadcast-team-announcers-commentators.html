AEW Broadcast Team: How to Stay Accurate When the Lineup Changes

If you’re trying to keep track of AEW’s announcers, commentators, and interviewers for recaps, podcasts, or fan debates, you’ve probably run into the same headache I have: the lineup changes all the time. AEW doesn’t stick to a fixed broadcast crew. Instead, roles shift weekly based on travel, health, storylines, and outside gigs. Memorizing last month’s list just leads to mistakes.

Here’s a direct guide for staying accurate, plus concrete examples and next steps that actually work.

Why Static Lists Fail

It’s tempting to pull up a “Who’s who” list and move on. But with AEW, that backfires. I once prepared show notes assuming Kevin Kelly would call Collision (he usually does). Turns out he was in Japan that week. The result? I gave out-of-date info and had to scramble for a correction after a friend called me on it. One quick check of that night’s episode or a trusted recap would have saved the hassle.

AEW treats the broadcast team as flexible, a strength for them, but a trap for us if we don’t double-check.

Current AEW Broadcast Roles: What You Can Expect (But Should Always Check)

Dynamite (Wednesday):

  • Excalibur: Almost always leads play-by-play.
  • Taz: Regular color commentator.
  • Tony Schiavone: Moves between commentary and backstage interviews, sometimes both in one night.
  • Jim Ross: Usually appears for big matches or pay-per-views.
  • Rotating guests: Sometimes wrestlers or managers join in for storyline reasons.

Collision (Saturday):

  • Nigel McGuinness: Main color commentator most weeks.
  • Kevin Kelly: Play-by-play unless he’s away (then Tony Schiavone fills in).
  • Ian Riccaboni or Caprice Coleman: Sub in if needed.

Rampage (Friday):

  • Excalibur leads when available.
  • Taz and/or Tony Schiavone often join, but this show rotates more than others.
  • Frequent guest spots based on stories or availability.

Backstage Interviewers:

  • Renee Paquette: Primary interviewer across shows.
  • Alex Marvez: Handles updates and midcard interviews; gets less screen time now.
  • Lexy Nair: Rampage and digital content specialist.
  • RJ City: Features mainly in YouTube segments (“Hey! EW”) with occasional TV cameos.
  • Dasha Gonzalez: Primarily Spanish-language ring announcing now; sometimes special event interviews.

If you need a tight summary for context, these are your “mainstays”, but treat them as starting points only.

Why People Get It Wrong

Most errors come from two traps:

  1. Assuming “the usual” team is present when someone is absent for travel or illness.
  2. Missing role swaps during an episode, like Tony Schiavone moving from ringside to backstage halfway through.

AEW is set up for flexibility. That makes static lists unreliable even week-to-week. A quick glance at Wikipedia or old press releases won’t catch these moves until well after the fact.

How To Get It Right Every Time

Skip generic advice like “just Google it.” Here are proven steps that work:

  1. Check the Latest Episode: Watch the first five minutes, announcers introduce themselves at the desk before any matches start. This is foolproof if you have access to TBS/YouTube/AEW Plus.
  2. Scan Trusted Recaps: If you’re short on time, use recaps from major wrestling news sites (Fightful, PWInsider, Voices of Wrestling) within 24 hours of broadcast, they list who was at the desk and interviewing that specific night.
  3. Pay-Per-View Caution: For PPVs and special events, expect hybrid teams (Jim Ross with Excalibur/Taz/Schiavone) and surprise guests. Always check post-show writeups before publishing anything about those broadcasts.

Tip: For evergreen articles (“Who are AEW’s current announcers?”), include a note saying lineups rotate frequently due to storylines and travel, and date your source check.

Special Note for International & Spanish-Language Coverage

AEW runs dedicated Spanish-language commentary teams on their international feeds, these names almost never appear in English recaps but matter if your audience is global.

Quick Checklist Before Publishing Content About AEW Broadcast Teams

  • Did I check this week’s intro segment for announcer assignments?
  • If not, did I read a recap dated within 24–48 hours of air?
  • For pay-per-view events, did I confirm any guest voices or unusual lineups?

Add this line to your notes:
“Lineup verified [date] via [source].”

This single habit has helped me avoid more errors than any cheat sheet could.

Encouragement & Next Steps

Don’t be discouraged if you trip up, it happens even to experienced fans. The trick is building a routine where you verify before publishing anything about who was at the desk or holding the mic backstage. Get used to quick cross-checks right before deadlines instead of relying on memory or last month’s wiki page.

When accuracy matters, and it does if your readers/listeners rely on you, these habits make all the difference. And if you ever get called out by a sharp-eyed friend (as I did), consider it part of learning how unpredictable wrestling can be behind the scenes.

Staying current with AEW’s broadcast team isn’t about knowing every name offhand; it’s about checking what happened this week so your work stands up under scrutiny. That attention to detail sets your coverage apart from everyone repeating outdated lists.

If you want help finding fast sources or decoding who was where on a particular show, just ask, I’m happy to point you toward specifics that save time (and embarrassment) down the line.
