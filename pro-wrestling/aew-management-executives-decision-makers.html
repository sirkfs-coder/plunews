If you want your proposal to land with the right people at All Elite Wrestling (AEW), knowing who’s in charge isn’t as simple as reading job titles. AEW is fast-moving and founder-driven, and real authority doesn’t always match what’s on the org chart.

Here’s a direct guide to navigating AEW’s management, who holds the keys, how decisions really get made, and where outreach usually goes off track.

Quick-Reference: AEW’s Core Decision Makers

NameRoleWhat They Decide
Tony KhanCEO, Head of CreativeFinal say on all major strategy, creative, partnerships, media deals
Megha ParekhChief Legal OfficerLegal approvals (contracts, rights, sponsorships)
Chris HarringtonSVP Strategy & RevenueAnalytics, business modeling, partnership feasibility
Dana MassieChief Marketing & Merchandising OfficerMerchandising, marketing collaborations

If your idea touches TV/streaming, talent, or big partnerships: it will hit Tony’s desk. If it needs a contract or legal review: Megha gets involved. Anything requiring business case analysis: expect Chris to probe your numbers. For merchandise/marketing collabs: start with Dana.

Why Titles Can Mislead (And Personal Experience With “False Gates”)

It’s tempting to think that any VP or senior manager can approve your deal. In wrestling, and especially at AEW, titles often mask where power actually sits. Here’s an illustrative example from outreach elsewhere in entertainment: I once spent weeks building rapport with a VP who seemed eager and engaged. Every meeting felt warm but nothing moved forward. Only later did I learn every real decision was held by the founder-CEO; my contact was essentially a friendly filter. The lesson? Watch for who has true authority versus who just manages incoming requests.

Signs You’ve Reached Real Decision Makers at AEW

Look for these cues in your interactions:

  • Forward-Looking Questions: When an executive asks how your proposal affects brand reach next quarter or fits into upcoming events, you’re speaking to someone who can shape outcomes.
  • Legal CCs: As soon as Megha Parekh or her team is included in replies, even just for “review”, your idea is advancing.
  • Tony Khan References: If responses mention needing Tony’s input or pause after big events (like TV tapings), you’re genuinely close to a decision.
  • Escalation Sequence: A reply chain that starts with Dana or Chris and then brings in legal means you’ve cleared initial hurdles.

Insider tip: Even one cc to Megha Parekh means things are getting real, don’t miss that cue!

Where Outreach Usually Fails

Three common traps trip up even experienced professionals:

  1. Assuming Quick Responses Mean Influence:
    If a VP answers right away but discussions stall (“We’ll circle back internally”), they might be polite gatekeepers, not ultimate deciders.

  2. Chasing “Backstage” Power Brokers:
    Unlike some wrestling companies where veterans pull strings behind the scenes, AEW keeps authority tightly centralized, nearly everything funnels through Tony or clear department heads.

  3. Using Outdated Contact Info:
    Roles shift at AEW without much public notice. Relying on last year’s LinkedIn connections or press mentions can sideline your pitch for months.

Step-by-Step Example: Pitching an AEW Collaboration

Suppose you represent an athletic wear brand wanting to launch co-branded merch. Here’s how a smart approach works:

  1. Targeted First Contact: Email Dana Massie directly about your vision; if possible, cc Chris Harrington to show you know who evaluates strategic fit.
  2. Show You’ve Done Your Homework: Reference their latest apparel drop (“I saw the recent Blackpool Combat Club line…”). This signals respect for their current strategy.
  3. Watch Response Patterns: Early mention of legal review? That means interest is real, now expect Megha Parekh’s office to step in.
  4. Monitor Timing: If discussions pause before formal terms are sent, or suddenly move fast after a big PPV, it likely reflects Tony Khan’s involvement around major company priorities.

If these patterns don’t appear (no escalation up the ladder, no legal involvement), it probably means your pitch hasn’t gained traction inside AEW yet.

How to Behave Like an Insider From Day One

  • Tailor every message to AEW’s current business priorities, reference specific events or projects so execs know you’re serious.
  • Use individual emails or LinkedIn; avoid generic info@ addresses that rarely go anywhere important.
  • Keep track of delays and align them with public AEW news cycles; this helps you tell if you’re waiting on reviews or being quietly declined.
  • After two weeks without movement following legitimate engagement (not just an auto-reply), follow up once more, with fresh information tied directly to their stated interests.

Before You Hit Send: Do This First

Spend 15 minutes checking recent AEW press releases and key executives’ LinkedIn updates for changes in roles or responsibilities. Make sure your outreach matches today’s structure, not last season’s headlines.

Final Encouragement

AEW isn’t impossible to reach, but their pace and hands-on leadership mean only the most focused pitches cut through. Study their recent moves, aim high on your first try, and track every signal as carefully as you would watching a live main event. If you see your email chain climbing from Dana Massie up through Chris Harrington and then over to Megha Parekh…you’re ringside for the real decision!

Don’t just knock, know whose door you’re knocking on. Good luck!
