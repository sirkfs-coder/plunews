Spotting Common Pitfalls When Tracking Former AEW Personnel

If you’ve ever tried to keep track of who’s still with AEW and who’s moved on, you know it’s not as simple as checking a roster page. AEW’s mix of on-screen roles, backstage changes, and the wrestling rumor mill all create plenty of room for mistakes. Over time, I’ve seen (and made) most of them. Here are the traps I watch out for now, and the steps that actually help.

Mistake: Assuming TV Absence Means Someone Has Left
It’s easy to think a wrestler is gone if they haven’t been on Dynamite in months. But with injuries, contract talks, or creative breaks, someone could be off TV but still under contract. As an illustrative example: I once marked a wrestler as “departed” after not seeing them for three months. They showed up at the next big event, same company, just a new look.

How to avoid this:

  • Check AEW’s official roster.
  • Look up recent results on Cagematch.net or similar databases to see if they’re working house shows or dark matches.
  • Scan news from Fightful, PWInsider, or AEW’s own press releases.
  • If in doubt, look at their social media for clues (but don’t treat rumors as facts).

Trap: Relying Too Heavily on Fan-Made Lists
Fan spreadsheets and wikis are great for quick checks but often lag behind real news. For example, several popular lists kept Cody Rhodes as an active EVP for weeks after he returned to WWE.

What works better:

  • Always check when a list was last updated.
  • Use the Wayback Machine to see if a resource has been slow to change.
  • Give more weight to sources with clear editorial oversight (not just anonymous edits).

Confusion Over Roles: On-Screen vs. Backstage
AEW loves multi-role staffers, someone can be a manager on TV and a coach backstage at the same time. This makes it easy to double-count departures or miss shifts entirely.

Practical tip:

  • Track both public and behind-the-scenes roles for each person you follow.
  • Confirm changes via official announcements or staff directories, not just what you see on TV.

Social Media Rumors: Sorting Fact From Fiction
Departure rumors explode after any backstage drama, CM Punk’s situation in 2023 is one example. These stories spread quickly before facts are confirmed.

Keep it clean by:

  • Waiting until at least two reputable outlets confirm the news.
  • Treating anything without direct evidence as “unverified,” no matter how convincing screenshots seem.

Inconsistent Definitions: Who Really Counts as “Former”?
Some lists include anyone who hasn’t been around lately; others only add wrestlers whose contracts have officially ended or who’ve appeared elsewhere. This muddles comparisons and research.

To stay consistent:

  • Define your criteria up front (e.g., only those with confirmed contract expirations or public exit statements).
  • Mark ambiguous cases (like “on hiatus” or “last seen”) instead of guessing.

Why Accurate Tracking Matters

Getting this wrong doesn’t just mess up your spreadsheet, it shapes how people see AEW’s booking trends and business moves. For instance, missing Cody Rhodes’ departure confused fans about leadership changes for months. Overcounting exits makes it look like everyone is leaving; missing big departures hides important shifts in direction.

A Reliable Tracking Workflow

Here’s what I do now:

  1. Build a Simple Sheet: Start with official sources for status. Add columns for last match date, role(s), reason for departure (if known), and where you got your info.
  2. Review Regularly: Set reminders to check official rosters monthly, especially after big events when exits spike.
  3. Flag Unclear Cases: Use tags like “rumored,” “on hiatus,” or “unconfirmed” instead of making hard calls without proof.
  4. Log Every Change: Note exactly when and why someone left (if known), with source links.
  5. Be Patient With Rumors: Wait for confirmation before updating your records, don’t get caught chasing every online whisper.

If You Make a Mistake

Don’t just fix it quietly, note what went wrong so you can spot similar issues faster next time. Was it slow updates? Misinformation? Logging these moments helps tighten your process over time (and helps others too).

Try This: Trace One Recent Departure

Pick someone high-profile like CM Punk or William Regal and walk through their exit step by step:

  • Double-check multiple sources confirming they left.
  • Note their last match date and all known AEW roles.
  • Record any public statements from AEW or the wrestler.
  • See where they landed next (if anywhere).
  • Save every link in your tracking sheet.

Doing this once will show you where information gaps pop up, and make your system more reliable going forward.

Final thought: Wrestling rosters are messy by nature, but some careful habits can save you hours of confusion down the line, and help everyone else looking for accurate info too.
