What I Wish I’d Known Before Training at Empire Wrestling Federation’s School

Thinking about starting at Empire Wrestling Federation’s School? If you’re like I was, you’re probably excited and a little nervous. Maybe you’ve watched wrestling for years, but stepping into a training ring is something else entirely. Here’s what actually happens inside those ropes, what gets most people tripped up, and how to make the most of your first year, without burning out or getting lost in the crowd.

Mastering Basics Matters More Than Fancy Moves

Most beginners arrive with visions of high-flying moves and dramatic slams. The reality is far less glamorous: the first months are all about learning to move safely. At Empire, this starts with “bump school”, drilling how to fall and get back up without injury. You’ll practice landing on your back, side, and front until your body learns to absorb shock instead of fighting it.

Illustrative example: On my second day, I tried to show off by jumping into a flashy flip. I landed flat, knocked the wind out of myself, and sprawled on the mat seeing stars. A trainer walked over and quietly said, “Let’s try that again, this time slower.” It was humbling. Most progress comes from nailing simple drills over and over.

Skip ahead too fast, and you’ll stall or end up hurt. Everyone wants to learn suplexes right away. The students who actually get there focus on footwork, balance, and safe landings first.

Who Succeeds? Not Just Athletes

You don’t need to look like a superhero to start at Empire. The only real requirements are basic health and enough stamina for push-ups, jogging laps, and stretching without pain. They’re strict about this, not to weed people out, but because wrestling is physically demanding in ways most people don’t expect.

If you have old injuries or can’t run a few laps without trouble, tell the trainers honestly during sign-up. They’ll give you prep routines instead of pushing you straight into drills that could make things worse.

Empire has students from all backgrounds: some were gym regulars; others hadn’t worked out in years. What separated those who stuck with it from those who dropped out wasn’t athleticism, it was consistency and taking feedback seriously.

Trainers Who Actually Teach (and Correct)

One fear I had was that trainers would be burnt-out pros just going through the motions. At Empire, most are still active on the local circuit, and they care about teaching well. Instead of just demonstrating moves once and moving on, they watch carefully while you drill each step.

The best trainers here break down why a move works or how it keeps you (and your opponent) safe. They don’t let sloppy habits slide, they’ll stop you mid-drill for corrections so problems don’t pile up later.

If you want real progress and fewer injuries, pick schools where coaches are hands-on every session rather than just letting everyone do their own thing.

No Promises, But Clear Ways to Get Noticed

Empire won’t guarantee anyone a contract with big promotions. What they do offer is structure: as you improve, there are student showcases where local promoters sometimes scout new talent. Getting picked isn’t about raw skill as much as reliability, showing up every week, learning from mistakes fast, and proving you can work safely with anyone.

Don’t expect shortcuts here; missing classes slows progress more than anything else.

What Your First Weeks Actually Look Like

Here’s what a typical beginner session felt like for me:

  • Warm-up (30–45 minutes): Jump rope until your legs burn; squats; push-ups; then more cardio.
  • Bump drills: Dozens of practice falls each class, back bumps, rolls, until muscle memory takes over.
  • Technical work: Footwork patterns across the ring; lock-ups; basic holds, all under close supervision.
  • Promo work: Group exercises where everyone tries short character speeches.
  • Cooldown/feedback: Trainers point out common errors (mine was always not tucking my chin enough) so you know exactly what to fix next time.

You won’t be doing matches right away, the early weeks build up coordination and confidence so nobody gets overwhelmed or hurt trying stunts too soon.

Common Pitfalls (and How Empire Helps You Avoid Them)

Three things trip up most beginners:

  1. Underestimating conditioning: Even if you lift weights or play other sports, wrestling workouts use muscles and endurance in unfamiliar ways. Expect soreness in odd places, rest days help.
  2. Taking critique personally: Trainers call out mistakes bluntly because safety depends on it, it’s never personal.
  3. Skipping sessions: Missing practices makes everything harder next time around. Students who disappear for weeks almost never catch up.

Composite example: One classmate missed three weeks with a minor sprain but didn’t stay in touch with trainers or follow recommended rehab exercises. When he came back, he felt behind everyone else, and left soon after.

Empire trainers encourage honest conversations about limits or setbacks so students don’t feel embarrassed asking for help early on.

What Success Looks Like at Empire

Not every graduate ends up on national TV, or even wants to. Some perform at local shows; others become referees or help run events behind the scenes. The ones who stand out aren’t always the strongest or flashiest, they’re relentless about showing up every week and asking questions after class rather than pretending they already know it all.

If your goal is building real skills in a supportive but challenging environment (not chasing fame overnight), Empire delivers exactly that.

How to Start Strong (Actual Next Steps)

  • Visit Empire’s official website for tuition details plus trainer bios.
  • Go to an open house or trial class before signing up fully, you’ll get a real sense of whether the vibe works for you.
  • Prepare three questions that matter most (like “How do beginners stay safe?” or “What support is there if I get injured?”).
  • Be upfront about your fitness level and goals when speaking with staff, they’ll set realistic timelines based on where you’re starting from.
  • Block off dedicated days each week for training sessions, consistency is more important than initial ability.

Final Advice: Don’t Wait For Perfect Timing

If wrestling has been calling your name for years, waiting until everything feels “just right” probably means waiting forever. The best move is visiting the school yourself, even if you feel unsure, and talking honestly with trainers about where you stand now versus where you want to go.

Every steady student I met started small: showing up regularly, focusing on basics even when it felt repetitive, staying humble enough to ask questions after class instead of quietly struggling alone.

Your first step isn’t signing a contract, it’s walking through those doors and saying hello. That single action puts your dream within reach faster than any amount of research ever could.
